How it works

Press D and keep watching

Rendering is not an export queue. Press D at any point and playback moves onto rendered frames a few seconds later, while the rest of the video renders behind you, nearest the playhead first.

Seek anywhere, rendered or not

Land on rendered frames and playback continues on them wherever they are on the timeline. Land somewhere nobody has rendered and the original plays at once while the render moves there. Nothing already rendered is thrown away.

Compare without moving the playhead

Split, wipe, side by side or 2 × 2, all on one timestamp. Difference shows where the model changed the picture, and the loupe magnifies both at the same spot, pixel for pixel. Change a neural setting while paused and that frame re-renders, so you decide on the picture.

Come back and it is still there

Renders stay on disk until you clear them or the drive falls below 20 GB free. Reopen a film weeks later and its whole-film render is still attached, reused only while the source, the runtime and the neural settings all still match, so what you reopen is what you rendered.

What it does

Every item here is in the usage guide, with the measurements behind it. Nothing is uploaded: the work happens on your GPU and the cache sits on your disk.

Compare

  • Split, wipe, side by side and 2 × 2, all on one timestamp
  • A Difference view of where the model changed the picture, amplified 1× to 32×
  • A loupe and zoom to 8×, down to single pixels
  • Hold the mouse on the picture to see the original in place
  • Save comparison image: exactly what is on screen, with a provenance footer

Play

  • Renders while you watch, and seeks anywhere, rendered or not
  • Local files, public YouTube links, photos, animated GIFs and six bundled game trailers
  • HDR10 and HLG tone-mapped for the model on the GPU, and the original shown in HDR on an HDR display
  • Subtitles (SRT, ASS, WebVTT, PGS, VobSub) drawn after the render, so text is never warped

Keep and export

  • A constant-quality render cache: HEVC at CQ 16 by default, 10-bit or lossless if you ask
  • PNG, JPEG, GIF, MP4 or MKV; MKV keeps the audio, subtitles and chapters untouched
  • Super Resolution, neural rendering and frame generation in one file, in NVIDIA’s order
  • Frame generation from 2× to 5× the frame rate, as a conversion
  • The same export from a script: --render, with exit codes

Check

  • A SHA-256 beside every zip, and what it does and does not prove
  • Signed build provenance on the core zip, checked with gh attestation verify
  • verify_package.ps1 checks every file against a pinned manifest before the first run
  • MIT licensed, built in the open on GitHub

Limits and requirements

The limits, stated as plainly as the features. Each is measured or documented in the repository.

HardwareAn NVIDIA RTX card and driver 610.47 or newer; older drivers are refused up front. Checked on an RTX 4080 SUPER and an RTX 5090. RTX 20 and 30 cards lack native FP8, should be several times slower, and are untested.

SpeedA live 1080p30 session costs 8.4 ms a frame on an RTX 5090 and 15.3 ms on an RTX 4080 SUPER. A whole 4K render on the 4080 SUPER ran at 14.8 fps, slower than a 30 fps film plays.

UpscalingDLSS Super Resolution scored below a plain bicubic upscale on all six video clips measured. It is built for games, so it is off by default and there for its look, not for detail.

RuntimeThe neural runtime is a modified, unsigned community build, not a signed NVIDIA package. Why it is unsigned.

ArtifactsDepth is estimated from the picture, and so is motion on cards without the optical flow engine. Expect some artifacts.

CacheA render started partway into a video is not joined to the rest, and below 20 GB free the least recently watched renders are deleted first.

Before you run it

  1. Unzip into a new, empty folder. Keep neural-runtime/ next to the executable.
  2. Run DLSSVideoPlayer.exe.
  3. Open a file with Ctrl+O, paste a YouTube link with Ctrl+L, or pick something from File > Game trailers.
  4. Press D. It buffers for a few seconds, then plays rendered.

This is a community project — it is not an NVIDIA product. The neural runtime it ships with is a modified, unsigned community build, so Windows and your antivirus will treat it as unknown software (why). Neural rendering needs NVIDIA driver 610.47 or newer, on an RTX card. Checked on an RTX 4080 SUPER and an RTX 5090.

GitHub's automatically generated “Source code” archives do not run: the runtime is not in them. Third-party notices are in THIRD_PARTY.md.

Questions

Is it safe to run?

It is open source and says what it contains, but part of it is unsigned, so decide with that in view. Each zip has a SHA-256 beside it. The core zip is built by CI from this repository and carries a signed build attestation (gh attestation verify); the complete zip is assembled by the maintainer and has a checksum only. Inside either, verify_package.ps1 checks every file against a pinned manifest before the first run. The exception is the neural runtime, a modified community build nobody can sign. Scan it as you would any unsigned software, or take the core zip and supply your own.

Why is the neural runtime unsigned?

Because it has been modified. It includes NVIDIA's neural DLL patched to run on RTX 40 cards, which invalidates NVIDIA's signature, alongside community ReShade and RenoDX files that were never signed. It is not a redistribution of a signed NVIDIA package and nobody is in a position to re-sign it, so it ships unsigned and says so. Its files are pinned by size and SHA-256, and the package verifier rejects anything that has drifted.

Which GPUs does it need?

An NVIDIA RTX card and driver 610.47 or newer for neural rendering; with an older driver the player says so up front. It has been checked on an RTX 4080 SUPER and an RTX 5090. RTX 20 and 30 series cards lack native FP8 and should be several times slower; nobody has tested one yet. The player itself opens and plays video without one; the render is what needs the hardware.

How long does a render take?

You do not wait for it to start: playback joins the render a few seconds after you press D, and the rest fills in while you watch. A live 1080p30 session costs 8.4 ms a frame on an RTX 5090 and 15.3 ms on an RTX 4080 SUPER, both faster than the video plays; 1440p30 on the 5090 costs 15.4 ms. A whole 4K film on the 4080 SUPER rendered at 14.8 frames a second. After your first session the player knows your GPU and warns you before a video it expects to fall behind on. The rest of the measured limits are above.

Does it upload my video anywhere?

No. Rendering runs on your GPU and the cache stays on your disk. The player goes online to fetch a YouTube link you paste, and once a day to ask GitHub whether there is a new release, which [Updates] Enabled=0 in its settings file turns off. The start screen also fetches the seven built-in trailers' thumbnails from YouTube's image server, then again once a month, which [Start] ThumbnailFetch=0 turns off.

Does it upscale video too?

It can, with DLSS Super Resolution, but that is off by default because on video it does not beat a plain scaler: from 960×540 to 1080p on six clips, a bicubic upscale scored higher VMAF every time. DLSS Super Resolution is built for rendered games, not decoded footage, so turn it on for its look, not for detail. Neural rendering itself keeps the source resolution.

Can I keep the result?

Yes: PNG or JPEG for photos, GIF for animations, MP4 or MKV for video, from the player or from a script with --render. MKV keeps the source audio, subtitles and chapters without re-encoding them. Export with DLSS stages writes Super Resolution, neural rendering and frame generation into one file, in NVIDIA's order.
